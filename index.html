Writing the letters: Roman numbers and variables

In math, a letter can stand for a number we do not know yet. That is called a variable, like x. Roman numbers and variables can look alike, so we write them differently:

  • Roman numbers are capital letters that stand straight up. Example: X is 10.
  • Variables are small letters that lean to the right. Example: x is a number we do not know yet.

The little feet on the ends of the letters are called serifs. They help you tell the letters apart, so write neatly and keep capital letters straight.

      Is this app how the Romans did it?

      Not exactly. This app breaks numbers into letters, so you can see what each letter is worth. The Romans mostly counted with small stones on a board, and wrote only the answer with letters.

      How the Romans did math

      They counted with small stones called calculi, Latin for "little stones". Each column is a place: tens, then ones.

      47: 4 stones in the tens column, 7 in the ones.

      Put the first number on the board. Then add the second number's stones to the same columns.

      23 + 14

      27 + 18: a trade

      7 + 8 is 15 ones. Ten ones trade for one ten.

      Take the stones away. If a column is short, trade one stone from the column before for ten in this one. Romans had no negative numbers, so you trade first.

      40 − 18

      Past 1000

      The Romans put a line over a letter to make it a thousand times bigger. An overlined I is 1,000, which is the same as M. That is why this app stops at M.

      No zero

      The Romans had no symbol for zero. In tables, they wrote the Latin word nulla, which means "none".

      No negative numbers

      A sum below zero had no Roman answer. Roman accounts kept money received (acceptum) and money spent (expensum) in two lists.

      IV or IIII?

      Both were used. The gates of the Colosseum use IIII for 4, but XL for 40. Clocks still use IIII. IV became the common form much later.

      Roman numbers through time

      1. About 2,000 years agoRomans carve numbers in letters on buildings and monuments.
      2. About 1,500 years agoIn India, people learn to write numbers with place value and zero.
      3. About 1,400 years agoBrahmagupta writes rules for doing math with zero (628 CE).
      4. About 800 years agoFibonacci brings these numbers to Europe (1202).
      5. TodayRoman numbers are still used for Super Bowls, clocks and book chapters.

      Where we still see them

      • Super Bowls. The NFL numbers them in Roman numerals. Super Bowl LIX was in February 2025.
      • Clocks. Many clock faces show IIII for 4.
      • Kings and queens. King Charles III.

      Our numbers came from India, with zero and place value. That is why they are easier to add and subtract on paper.

      Multiplying with letters

      Adding and taking away are easy with letters, but multiplying is much harder. Nobody has found a Roman paper method for it. Most historians think Romans multiplied on the counting board, using pebbles and beads, and perhaps by doubling and halving.

      Our place-value numbers changed that. Each column holds one digit, so you can write the whole method on paper, column by column.

      Historians still argue about exactly how Romans multiplied. The board is the best evidence; doubling is a likely guess.

      From the Roman way to our way

      Counting board (stones)Place value (ours)
      Where it was doneOn a board, with pebblesOn paper, with digits
      Empty placeA blank line on the boardZero
      MultiplyingPebble by pebble, or by doublingA grid of small products (the lattice method)

      The Indian system with zero reached Europe through Arabic mathematicians. Fibonacci's book of 1202 helped spread it. The grid for multiplying (the lattice method) came the same way, from India through Arab and Persian mathematicians. Fibonacci's book did not start it.

      Other ways to write numbers

      • The Maya (Central America). They counted in twenties, with a dot for one, a bar for five, and a shell for zero. Some say their zero was invented before the Indian one, but the dates are uncertain.
      • China. Counting rods were laid out on a board, and a place on the board gave the value. A blank space meant zero. They were used from long before Christ into the 1600s.

      Did the Romans multiply?

      Most historians think yes. They multiplied on the counting board with pebbles, and maybe by doubling and halving. We do not know of a Roman way to multiply on paper with letters.

      For the learner: why we are doing this

      Roman numerals are letters people used for counting long ago. You already know some, like I, V and X. In this lesson you will read them, fix them when they are wrong, and add and subtract them, without changing them into our numbers.
