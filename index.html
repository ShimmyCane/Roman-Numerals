Rule 1

Equal letters in a row are added. No more than three in a row.

III = 3
XXX = 30
IIII → IV
XXXX → XL

Rule 2

A smaller letter on the right of a bigger one is added. No more than three.

VI = 6
XVIII = 18
VIIII → IX

Rule 3

A smaller letter on the left of a bigger one is taken away. Only one, never a few.

IV = 4
XL = 40
CM = 900
IIX → IX only one I before the X

Rule 5

Always write it in the simplest form.

VV → X
LL → C
IIII → IV

So is this app how the Romans did it?

Not exactly. Breaking numbers into single letters and cancelling them is a teaching model. It helps you see what each letter is worth. The Romans mostly did their sums on a counting board and wrote only the answer in letters.

How did they count?

On a counting board or a hand abacus, moving small stones called calculi (Latin for "little stones") across columns. Each column stood for a place: tens, ones, and so on. The answer was written down in numerals at the end.

A surviving hand abacus has seven columns. Each column has four beads in its lower slot and one bead in its upper slot.

47 on a counting board: 4 pebbles in the tens, 7 in the ones.

How did they add?

Put the first number on the board, then add the second number's pebbles to the same columns. A reconstruction of the method, not a recorded Roman procedure, but it is how a counting board works.

Example 1: 23 + 14

Example 2: 27 + 18 (a trade in the ones)

7 + 8 makes 15 ones. Ten ones swap for one ten.

How did they subtract?

Take the pebbles away from the board. When a column does not have enough, trade one pebble from the next column up for ten in this one. Romans had no negative numbers, so you trade first.

Example: 40 − 18

Did they have zero?

No. Roman numerals had no symbol for zero. In tables they wrote the Latin word nulla ("none") instead. In 525 Dionysius Exiguus used nulla this way in his Easter tables.

Did they have negative numbers?

No. Roman arithmetic had no negative numbers, so a sum that would go below zero had no Roman answer. Roman accounts kept money received (acceptum) and money spent (expensum) as separate lists. Scholars still debate how far they balanced these.

Did they write IV and IX?

Both forms appear. The Colosseum's numbered gates use IIII for 4 but XL for 40, so gate 44 is XLIIII. Clock faces still use IIII today. The IV and IX style became the usual form much later.

Trivia

  • Medieval England's Exchequer, the government money office, took its name from a checkered cloth on a table. Counters on the squares stood for pounds, shillings and pence.
  • The NFL numbers its Super Bowls in Roman numerals. Super Bowl LIX was played in February 2025.
  • Kings and queens still use them: King Charles III, and Queen Elizabeth II before him.
  • Roman numerals are still used on clock faces, in book chapters and outlines, and in movie copyright dates.

Where our numbers came from: Hindu-Arabic numerals

The 1, 2, 3 we use today came from India, not from Rome. Two ideas made them work: zero and place value, where a digit's position sets its size.

  • By about 500 CE: Indian mathematicians write numbers with place value.
  • 628 CE: Brahmagupta writes the first rules for calculating with zero.
  • About 825: al-Khwarizmi describes the Indian system in a book, and the word "algorithm" comes from his name.
  • 1202: Fibonacci's Liber Abaci introduces the system to European merchants.
