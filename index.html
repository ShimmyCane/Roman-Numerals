    How to read this

    If the letters can just slide together, that is one step.

    If not, the numbers are lined up by place, like the columns of a counting board: thousands, hundreds, tens, ones. Each step changes one place, starting with the ones.

    When a place reaches ten, one letter moves up to the next place. When a place is short, it borrows one letter from the place above.

    This is a teaching model, built like a counting board.

    Is this app how the Romans did it?

    Not exactly. This app breaks numbers into letters, so you can see what each letter is worth. The Romans mostly counted with small stones on a board, and wrote only the answer with letters.

    How the Romans did math

    They counted with small stones called calculi, Latin for "little stones". Each column is a place: tens, then ones.

    47: 4 stones in the tens column, 7 in the ones.

    Put the first number on the board. Then add the second number's stones to the same columns.

    23 + 14

    27 + 18: a trade

    7 + 8 is 15 ones. Ten ones trade for one ten.

    Take the stones away. If a column is short, trade one stone from the column before for ten in this one. Romans had no negative numbers, so you trade first.

    40 − 18

    Past 1000

    The Romans put a line over a letter to make it a thousand times bigger. An overlined I is 1,000, which is the same as M. That is why this app stops at M.

    No zero

    The Romans had no symbol for zero. In tables, they wrote the Latin word nulla, which means "none".

    No negative numbers

    A sum below zero had no Roman answer. Roman accounts kept money received (acceptum) and money spent (expensum) in two lists.

    IV or IIII?

    Both were used. The gates of the Colosseum use IIII for 4, but XL for 40. Clocks still use IIII. IV became the common form much later.

    Roman numbers through time

    1. About 2,000 years agoRomans carve numbers in letters on buildings and monuments.
    2. About 1,500 years agoIn India, people learn to write numbers with place value and zero.
    3. About 1,400 years agoBrahmagupta writes rules for doing math with zero (628 CE).
    4. About 800 years agoFibonacci brings these numbers to Europe (1202).
    5. TodayRoman numbers are still used for Super Bowls, clocks and book chapters.

    Where we still see them

    • Super Bowls. The NFL numbers them in Roman numerals. Super Bowl LIX was in February 2025.
    • Clocks. Many clock faces show IIII for 4.
    • Kings and queens. King Charles III.
