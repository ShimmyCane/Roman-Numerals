How to read this

The checker names each rule the numeral breaks. The steps show how it gets repaired.

Rule 1

Equal letters in a row are added. No more than three in a row.

III = 3
IIII ✗

Rule 2

A smaller letter on the right of a bigger one is added. No more than three.

VI = 6
XVIII = 18
VIIII ✗

Rule 3

A smaller letter on the left of a bigger one is taken away. Only one, never a few.

IV = 4
XL = 40
IIV ✗

Always write it in the simplest form.

Did Romans use Roman numerals?

Yes. They wrote numbers on buildings, tombs and monuments with these letters.

How did they do math?

Mostly on a counting board, moving small pebbles (calculi, Latin for "little pebbles") in columns. The numerals were used to write down the answer.

Did they write IV and IX?

Sometimes. But IIII and VIIII are also found on Roman inscriptions and clock faces. The IV and IX style became the usual form much later.

Did they have zero?

No zero letter. There was no Roman numeral for zero.

So is this app how Romans did it?

Not exactly. Breaking numbers into pieces is a teaching model to see what each letter is worth. It helps you learn the numbers, even if it is not the way the Romans worked them out.
